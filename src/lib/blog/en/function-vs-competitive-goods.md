# Function goods and competitive goods: why abundance does not eliminate inequality

## The premise

A common intuition says that once AI is capable enough, all software and every automatable service will become practically free, and a substantial part of economic inequality will disappear with them. This intuition is correct, but **only for half of all goods**.

The decisive distinction is not how technically hard a good is to produce, but **how its success is defined**. There are two possible definitions, and they exclude each other:

- A good whose success is measured against an **absolute threshold**: does it work or not?
- A good whose success is measured against **everyone else**: is it better than theirs?

These two categories behave in radically different ways under abundant artificial intelligence. The first collapses to marginal cost. The second never does.

## Type 1: function goods

These are products or services that are only expected to **do a job**, and whose performance is not tied to the profitability or competitive position of whoever uses them.

**Examples:** a small business's management app, a restaurant's website, a booking form, an internal inventory tool, an administrative procedure, an association's accounting software, a script that sorts files.

The acceptance criterion for these goods is **binary and saturable**. The booking is recorded or it isn't; the invoice is issued or it isn't. Once the threshold of correct operation is crossed, extra quality adds no appreciable marginal value: a booking app twice as good does not produce twice the bookings, because the ceiling is set not by the app but by the business's demand.

Three chained consequences follow:

1. **Any solution that works is enough.** There is no incentive to pay for a superior version, because "superior" means nothing here once the threshold is reached.
2. **The marginal cost of production is zero.** If AI can generate it on demand, the cost of the next copy — adapted, specific, tailor-made — is that of inference itself, which tends to zero.
3. **A positive price is unsustainable.** When the good is saturable and its replica is free, no provider can hold a price above the cost of generating it again from scratch.

**Conclusion:** in this category the prophecy of abundance comes true literally. **Everyone gets, free and tailored, what today costs tens of thousands of euros in development.** The surplus is entirely socialised. A whole industry — bespoke software and standardised management — is destroyed, and the benefit is spread diffusely among all its former customers.

## Type 2: competitive goods

These are products or services that play a central role in a commercial activity and, by their very nature, **compete against other people's**. Their performance is not measured in absolute terms, but by **how much better they are than the rival's**.

**Examples:** a trading bot, a dynamic-pricing system, a logistics resource-allocation engine, an ad-bidding algorithm, a fraud-detection system, a drug-discovery tool, or any performance optimiser in a contested sector.

There is no saturation threshold here, because the bar is not fixed: **the bar is everyone else's percentile.** A trading bot isn't "good"; it is good *relative to* whatever is on the other side of the trade. If everyone improves, no one improves.

Consider a concrete case: a user asks an AI "give me a trading bot to generate income" and spends $200 of compute and energy in that session. Their bot is not facing an abstract problem: it is facing the bots of every other user who did the same. Some spent $10. Others spent $20,000. The relevant question is no longer whether their bot works, but **where in that distribution it lands**.

Four consequences follow:

1. **Compute becomes competitive advantage directly.** Inference spending stops being an operating cost and becomes an investment in relative position. What you buy is not the tool; it is the percentile.
2. **The market degenerates into a compute auction.** If performance is an increasing function of the resources invested, every participant has an incentive to scale spending up to the point where expected marginal benefit equals marginal cost. Competition shifts from talent to capital.
3. **Red Queen effect.** As in the classic arms race, aggregate spending grows without limit while relative positions stay unchanged. Everyone runs faster, no one advances.
4. **Surplus dissipation.** In zero-sum cases — trading is the pure example — collective spending on compute creates no social value: it only redistributes what exists, consuming energy and silicon in the process. The whole sector can end up burning billions to reallocate the same rents.

**Conclusion:** in this category, AI abundance **does not reduce inequality: it rebuilds it on a new base.** Access to the model is democratised, but access to *compute* is not. And when the quality of the solution is a monotonic function of the compute invested, the advantage of capital returns intact, only now expressed in FLOPs instead of factories.

## The variable that decides everything: the shape of the curve

The whole argument above rests on a single empirical unknown, which is probably **the most important economic variable of the coming decade**:

> **What shape is the curve linking compute invested to quality of result?**

There are two possibilities, and the social consequences are opposite:

- **Curve with early saturation (strongly diminishing returns).** If beyond a certain point spending a hundred times more produces a result barely 5% better, then the $200 user ends up at a negligible distance from the $20,000 one. The advantage of capital becomes marginal, Type 2 behaves *de facto* like Type 1, and abundance is genuinely equalising. This scenario pushes toward a **paradigm of decentralised competition**.
- **Curve without saturation (sustained returns, even if logarithmic).** If each order of magnitude of compute buys a consistent improvement, and that improvement translates into market capture, then concentration is mathematically inevitable: whoever spends most wins most; whoever wins most can spend most. This scenario pushes directly toward a **paradigm of centralised control** and, with it, toward **techno-feudalism**.

Note what this implies: **who holds power may not be a political decision, but the mechanical consequence of a technical fact.** If the curve doesn't saturate, no regulation of access to the models prevents concentration, because the scarce thing is not the model but the compute. And if it saturates, decentralisation happens by gravity, almost regardless of what the legislator decides.

## Two second-order dynamics

**1. Migration from Type 1 to Type 2.** The boundary between the two categories is not fixed. When a function good becomes universally free and every competitor has it, differentiation moves to another dimension — and that new dimension becomes the new competitive ground. A restaurant that today stands out by having a decent website will tomorrow stand out for nothing on that account, and competition will shift to its dynamic-pricing system, its purchasing optimisation or its reputation management. **Competition does not disappear: it moves to the next unsaturated link.** Every price collapse in Type 1 creates a new front in Type 2.

**2. Inequality becomes positional, not material.** The combined result of both categories is a world where functional needs are met for everyone at zero cost, and where at the same time competition for relative positions is more intense and more expensive than ever. It is a regime in which **nobody lacks anything, and yet the distance between first and last has only grown.** No redistribution model based on guaranteeing access to material goods — universal basic income first among them — addresses this form of inequality, because what is contested is not a good: it is a place. If everyone receives the same, everyone can invest the same in compute, and the percentiles stay exactly where they were. Universal basic income is effective against scarcity and essentially inert against positional inequality.

## Side-by-side summary

| | **Type 1: function goods** | **Type 2: competitive goods** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Success criterion** | Absolute: does it work? | Relative: better than the rival? |
| **Does it saturate?** | Yes, at the threshold of usefulness | No, the bar moves by itself |
| **Marginal cost** | Tends to zero | Tends to zero per unit, but aggregate spending tends to infinity |
| **Effect of extra spending** | None | Decisive |
| **Fate of the surplus** | Socialised among users | Concentrated in whoever owns the compute |
| **Effect on inequality** | Reduces it | Reproduces it on a new base |
| **Pushes power toward** | Decentralisation | Centralisation |

## Conclusion: two simultaneous economies

Advanced AI does not produce one economy but two, superimposed and governed by incompatible logics.

In the first, that of **function goods**, the promise of abundance is fulfilled in full: zero marginal cost and the absence of any advantage from overspending make the good effectively free and universal. It is the economy of post-scarcity, and it is real.

In the second, that of **competitive goods**, it is not fulfilled at all: the relative criterion prevents saturation, turns compute into capital and reproduces inequality with an efficiency possibly greater than that of the current system, because silicon is more concentrable and more mobile than a factory.

The most frequent analytical error — present in both technological optimism and catastrophism — is to extrapolate the logic of one of these economies to the whole. Whoever looks at Type 1 announces the end of scarcity; whoever looks at Type 2 announces definitive feudalism. Both correctly describe one half of the phenomenon, and both are wrong to present it as the whole.

The future will therefore not be decided on the ground of function goods, where the outcome is already written. It will be decided **in how the compute that feeds competitive goods is shared out** — and, quite possibly, in whether the returns curve saturates or not.

## Where Celaut comes in

So far, the general argument. What follows is our reading of how one specific project relates to it, and it should be read as such.

Celaut is a peer-to-peer architecture for designing and distributing software: reproducible services that any node can run, advertise or pay for, with no central authority. Set beside the two economies, it touches both at four points.

**1. Function goods are what a network like this distributes at close to zero cost.** A service is a reproducible, content-addressed unit; once published, any node can run it. The Unstoppable Skills registry also lets you search by the problem rather than by the provider, with benchmarks and reputation. That fits Type 1: when all that matters is that something works, what you need is to find it and run it, not to buy it.

**2. Compute is the scarce resource of Type 2, and Celaut is, among other things, a compute market.** Anyone can [rent out their machine](/depin) when they aren't using it and price it around what their electricity costs; nodes negotiate cost between peers and settle in Ergo or Bitcoin. If the scarce thing isn't the model but the compute, it matters that compute be offered in an open market that needs no permission to enter, and not only in the catalogues of a handful of providers. Renting capacity in bursts is also a way for a small player to compete without owning silicon.

**3. Game of Prompts is a Type 2 arena built on purpose.** On that platform, made of Celaut services, creators design game-services that evaluate robots, and players write solver-services that try to maximise their score; results and prizes are recorded on Ergo. It is exactly the trading-bot scenario: a solver's value is its position against the others, and the compute spent on it has a visible effect on that position. It works as a small, measurable laboratory for the curve everything depends on: does doubling compute improve the result a little, or a lot?

**4. Reputation matters more in Type 2 than in Type 1.** With a function good you can check for yourself whether it works. With a competitive one, knowing whether a service is really better than another takes comparable measurements and a track record that is costly to fake. Celaut's design, with a reputation layer in which nobody spams without risking something, points that way, though it is one piece and not a solution.

### What Celaut does not solve

It would be dishonest to present it as an answer to the problem. Celaut **does not change the shape of the curve**, and if the curve doesn't saturate, whoever has more capital will still buy more compute, in an open market too. Nor does it correct positional inequality: opening the market lowers the barriers to entry and keeps compute from depending on a handful of providers, but it doesn't stop the biggest spender from winning most. What it can do is push in the decentralising direction: infrastructure with no owner, function goods circulating as a commons, and the contest for positions fought on ground where taking part needs no permission.

If it is true that the future is decided in how compute is shared out, then building an open network where that compute is offered, paid for and verified between peers is not an engineering detail. It is a bet on one of the two outcomes.
